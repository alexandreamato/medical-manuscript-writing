# Writing in English as a Non-Native Author

Most medical authors worldwide write in a second language. Editors and reviewers forgive an accent; they do not forgive ambiguity. The goal is plain, correct English that says exactly what was done and found. This guide focuses on Portuguese-speaking authors, but most points apply to speakers of other Romance languages.

## 1. Principles

1. **Write short sentences.** One idea per sentence, about 15 to 25 words. Long Portuguese sentences with several subordinate clauses become unreadable in English.
2. **Put the subject and the verb first.** "Statin users had fewer ulcers" rather than "It was observed that, among the patients who used statins, there was a lower number of ulcers".
3. **Prefer verbs to nouns.** "We measured" rather than "The measurement was performed"; "compression reduced oedema" rather than "a reduction of oedema was observed with compression".
4. **Use the active voice when the actor matters** ("We randomized"), and the passive when it does not ("Blood was drawn at 8 am").
5. **Be literal.** Idioms, irony, and rhetorical questions rarely survive translation.

## 2. False Friends Common in Medical Writing

| Portuguese | Wrong in English | Use instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| atualmente | actually (= in fact) | currently, at present |
| eventualmente | eventually (= in the end) | occasionally, possibly |
| pretender | pretend (= fake) | intend, aim |
| sensível | sensible (= reasonable) | sensitive |
| requintado, primoroso | exquisite (overused, often wrong) | excellent, detailed; or delete |
| colaborar com (dados, resultados) | collaborate with the results | contribute to, support |
| assistir (o paciente) | assist | treat, care for |
| realizar (um exame) | realize (= become aware) | perform, carry out, or use the verb: "we scanned" |
| apresentar (sintoma) | present (overused) | have, report, develop |
| evidenciar | evidence (as a verb) | show, reveal |
| patologia (a doença) | pathology (= the study or the lesion) | disease, condition |
| sintomatologia | symptomatology | symptoms |
| casuística | casuistry (= moral reasoning) | study population, case series, sample |
| controlar (monitorar) | control (= adjust, in statistics) | monitor, follow; keep "controlled for" for statistical adjustment |

## 3. Grammar Patterns That Interfere

1. **Articles.** English omits the article with general plural or uncountable nouns: "Patients with diabetes" (not "The patients with diabetes") when meaning patients in general; use "the" for a specific group already mentioned ("the patients in this cohort").
2. **"It is observed that", "It is known that", "It is important to note that".** Delete them and state the fact with its citation.
3. **Comma splices.** Two independent clauses joined by a comma ("The ulcer healed, the patient was discharged") need a full stop, a semicolon, or a conjunction.
4. **Decimal separator.** English uses the decimal point (0.72), not the comma (0,72); thousands take a comma or nothing (1500 or 1,500, as the journal prefers). Check every number when translating tables.
5. **Dates and units.** Write dates unambiguously (12 March 2026) and follow SI units with a space (5 mg, 37 °C).
6. **Adjective order and noun stacks.** "Chronic venous disease patients follow-up protocol" is a stack; write "the follow-up protocol for patients with chronic venous disease".
7. **Tense.** Past tense for what you did and found (Methods, Results); present tense for established knowledge and for what the table shows ("Table 1 shows").
8. **"Evaluate", "assess", "analyse" overuse.** Say what was actually done: measured, scanned, counted, compared, interviewed.
9. **"Besides", "moreover", "furthermore" at every sentence start.** Most can be deleted; the logical link should be clear from the content.
10. **Person-first language**: "patients with diabetes", not "diabetics"; "people with obesity", not "the obese".

## 4. Strategies

1. **Write the argument first, in the language you think in**, as an outline of one-sentence paragraphs. Translate the outline, then expand in English. Translating finished Portuguese prose sentence by sentence carries its structure over.
2. **Learn from published papers in the target journal.** Reusing standard, generic phrasing ("Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1", "Data are presented as mean and standard deviation") is normal and acceptable. Copying sentences that express someone else's ideas, arguments, or findings is plagiarism, even with a citation; paraphrase and cite.
3. **Keep a personal glossary** of the terms and phrases you use often, checked once against good sources (MeSH, journal style guides).
4. **Professional language editing** before submission helps when the English is a barrier; some journals list services, and some societies offer discounts. Editing does not change authorship or responsibility.
5. **AI-assisted language editing** is widely used. The authors remain responsible for every sentence, must check that meaning, numbers, and citations were not altered, and must follow the journal's disclosure policy: some journals require declaring any AI use, others exempt pure language editing (the Jornal Vascular Brasileiro exempts language editing and translation). See `references/ethics-and-integrity.md`.
6. **Read it aloud.** A sentence you cannot read in one breath is too long.

## 5. Self-Check Before Submission

1. No sentence longer than about 30 words without a reason.
2. No "it is observed that", "it is important to note that".
3. False friends checked (Section 2), especially actually, eventually, sensible, pathology, casuistry.
4. Decimal points, not commas, in text, tables, and figures.
5. Articles checked in the abstract and the first paragraph of each section.
6. Every "evaluate/assess" replaced by the specific action where possible.
7. Tense consistent: past for this study, present for established facts.
8. Language editing or AI assistance disclosed if the journal requires it.
