# Title, Running Title, and Keywords (Medical Manuscripts)

The title is the part of the paper most people will ever read, and the part databases index first. It must tell a searcher what was studied, in whom, and with what design, in words they would type into PubMed.

## 1. What a Medical Title Must Contain

1. **The question's core elements**: population or condition, intervention or exposure, comparator when there is one, and outcome when it fits.
2. **The design**, in a commonly used term. Reporting guidelines require it:

| Guideline | Item | Requirement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CONSORT 2025 | 1a | Identification as a randomised trial in the title. |
| STROBE | 1(a) | The study's design with a commonly used term in the title or abstract (the title is the better place). |
| PRISMA 2020 | 1 | Identify the report as a systematic review (and meta-analysis when there is one). |
| STARD 2015 | 1 | Identification as a study of diagnostic accuracy, using at least one measure of accuracy, in the title or abstract. |
| CARE | 1 | The diagnosis or intervention of primary focus, followed by the words "case report". |

3. **Nothing the paper does not deliver.** A title that promises an effect the design cannot establish is the first overstatement a reviewer sees.

## 2. Descriptive, Declarative, or Question

| Type | Example (fictional) | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Descriptive | `Statin use and incident venous leg ulcer: a prospective cohort study` | Default. Says what was studied, not what was found. Accepted everywhere. |
| Declarative | `Statins reduce venous leg ulcer recurrence: a randomized trial` | Only when the journal allows it and the design supports a causal claim (a trial with a clear primary result). Avoid for observational studies. |
| Question | `Do statins prevent venous leg ulcer?` | Rarely; some journals use them for commentaries or editorials. The answer must be in the abstract. |

What the evidence says:

1. Journal policies differ. Many journals prohibit declarative titles and a few encourage them. In a randomized study of 144 readers, a declarative title did not increase confidence in a trial's conclusion compared with a descriptive one (Wager et al., 2016). This does not make them harmless for weaker designs; it means the choice should follow the journal and the design, not a belief that declarative titles persuade.
2. Among highly and poorly cited articles in general and specialist medical journals, longer titles, titles with a colon, and titles with an acronym were associated with more citations, and naming a specific country with fewer (Jacques and Sebire, 2010). This is an observational association in a small sample; use it to justify a complete, searchable title, not to add acronyms.

## 3. Length and Form

1. **Check the journal's limit first.** Limits are given in characters (with spaces) or words; some journals have none. When there is no limit, aim for about 10 to 20 words.
2. **Two-part titles with a colon** work well: the question first, the design after the colon (`Topic in population: a randomized controlled trial`).
3. **No abbreviations** except universally known ones (DNA, HIV), and some journals ban even those. Spell out on first and only use.
4. **Avoid empty words**: "A study of", "Investigation into", "Observations on", "Novel", "First", "New insights". Novelty is judged by readers; claiming it invites the reviewer to prove it wrong.
5. **Avoid country names** unless the setting is the point of the study (a national registry, a health-system comparison).
6. **Use the terms people search**: the MeSH or DeCS term for the condition in the title makes the paper findable. Prefer `chronic venous insufficiency` over a local synonym.
7. **Match the abstract and the text**: the population, the intervention, and the outcome named in the title must be the ones reported as primary.

## 4. Running Title (Short Title)

1. A shortened title used in page headers; journals usually cap it at 40 to 60 characters with spaces (Jornal Vascular Brasileiro: 50, in the manuscript's language, no abbreviations).
2. Keep the condition and the intervention or exposure; drop the design and the population details.
3. Do not introduce an abbreviation to make it fit.

## 5. Keywords

1. Choose 3 to 6 keywords (or the journal's number) that are **not already in the title**: databases index the title anyway, so keywords add search entry points.
2. Take them from a controlled vocabulary: **MeSH** (https://meshb.nlm.nih.gov) for English-language journals, **DeCS** (https://decs.bvsalud.org) for Brazilian and Latin American journals. DeCS gives the Portuguese, Spanish, and English forms of the same descriptor, which keeps bilingual keyword lists consistent.
3. Include the design as a keyword when the journal asks (for example, "Cohort Studies", "Case Reports").
4. Check each term exists in the vocabulary; a keyword that is not a descriptor is often discarded.

## 6. Titles in Two Languages

Journals such as the Jornal Vascular Brasileiro require the title in Portuguese and English (and abstracts and keywords in both).

1. Translate the meaning, not the word order. Portuguese titles are often longer; the English one does not need to mirror it word by word.
2. Keep the design term equivalent in both (`estudo de coorte prospectivo` / `prospective cohort study`).
3. Use the DeCS descriptor in each language for the condition.
4. Check that neither version adds a claim the other does not make.

## 7. Before and After (Fictional Examples)

| Before | Problem | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `A study of statins in venous disease` | Empty opener; no outcome, no design | `Statin use and incident venous leg ulcer in chronic venous disease: a prospective cohort study` |
| `Statins prevent venous ulcers` | Causal claim from an observational study | `Statin use and venous leg ulcer incidence: a prospective cohort study` |
| `Novel use of foam sclerotherapy: first report in Brazil` | "Novel", "first", and country as selling points | `Ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy for recurrent varicose veins after surgery: a case series` |
| `EVLA vs RFA for GSV incompetence` | Abbreviations only | `Endovenous laser versus radiofrequency ablation for great saphenous vein reflux: a randomized trial` |
| `Evaluation of a new score` | No condition, no population, no design | `Accuracy of the Example Thrombosis Score for deep vein thrombosis in emergency department patients: a diagnostic accuracy study` |
| `Lipedema: a review` | No scope, no method | `Diagnostic criteria for lipedema: a systematic review` |
| `Unusual case of a venous aneurysm` | Diagnosis vague, "case report" missing | `Popliteal venous aneurysm presenting as recurrent pulmonary embolism: a case report` |
| `Effect of compression on quality of life in patients with venous ulcers treated at a university hospital in São Paulo` | Setting overloaded; outcome after the population | `Compression therapy and quality of life in venous leg ulcer: a cross-sectional study` |
| `Is duplex scanning necessary?` | Question with no subject and no design | `Routine duplex ultrasound before varicose vein surgery and recurrence at 5 years: a cohort study` |

## 8. Title Checklist

1. Population or condition, intervention or exposure, and design are named.
2. The design term is the one the reporting guideline expects.
3. No claim beyond what the design supports; no "novel" or "first".
4. No abbreviations (or only those the journal accepts).
5. Within the journal's limit; running title within its limit.
6. Keywords are controlled-vocabulary terms not already in the title.
7. Both language versions say the same thing.
