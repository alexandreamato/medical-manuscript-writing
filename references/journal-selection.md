# Choosing a Journal, Avoiding Predatory Journals, and Preprints

Choose the journal before writing the final draft: its article types, word limits, reference style, and readership shape the manuscript. Changing journal after a rejection is normal; plan a first choice and two alternatives.

## 1. Fit

1. **Scope and article type.** Read the aims and scope and the list of article types. A cohort study sent to a journal that publishes mainly trials, or a case report to a journal that no longer accepts them, is desk-rejected.
2. **Readership.** Who needs the finding to act on it: a specialty audience (vascular surgeons), a general medical audience, or a regional one (a Brazilian journal when the finding concerns local practice or population).
3. **Recent content.** Search the journal for the last 2 to 3 years on your topic. Related recent articles show interest; they also tell you what you must cite and how your study differs.
4. **Requirements you can meet.** Word and reference limits, bilingual abstracts, reporting checklists, data-sharing statements, trial registration before enrolment.

## 2. Indexing

Indexing decides whether readers will find the paper.

| Index | What it means | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MEDLINE | Selected by NLM; indexed with MeSH in PubMed | NLM Catalog: "Currently indexed for MEDLINE" (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nlmcatalog) |
| PubMed Central (PMC) | Full-text archive; being in PMC puts articles in PubMed, not necessarily in MEDLINE | NLM Catalog and the PMC journal list |
| Scopus, Web of Science | Commercial citation databases | Their source lists |
| SciELO | Latin American and Caribbean open-access collection with its own selection criteria | https://www.scielo.org |
| LILACS | Latin American and Caribbean health literature (BIREME/PAHO) | BVS / LILACS journal list |
| DOAJ | Directory of open-access journals meeting its criteria | https://doaj.org |

A journal's claim of indexing must be checked at the source; "indexed in Google Scholar" or an unfamiliar "impact factor" is not indexing.

## 3. Costs, Access, and Licence

1. **Article processing charges (APCs)** vary from none (some society and SciELO journals; the Jornal Vascular Brasileiro charges no submission or publication fee) to several thousand dollars. Check waivers for low- and middle-income countries and institutional agreements.
2. **Open access and licence.** CC BY is the most common open licence and is required by many funders. Check the licence before signing the copyright form.
3. **Funder mandates.** Some funders require immediate open access or deposit in a repository; confirm the journal's policy allows it.
4. **Turnaround.** Time to first decision and to publication are often published by the journal; ask colleagues who published there recently.

## 4. Predatory Journals

Predatory journals and publishers put self-interest ahead of scholarship, and are characterized by false or misleading information, deviation from best editorial and publication practices, a lack of transparency, and/or aggressive and indiscriminate solicitation (a consensus definition, Grudniewicz et al., 2019). Publishing there wastes the data and can harm the authors' reputation.

Warning signs:

1. Unsolicited e-mails inviting submission, often flattering and outside your field.
2. Promise of very fast peer review or acceptance.
3. Indexing or impact-factor claims that do not check out at the source.
4. Journal name imitating a well-known title; editorial board members who do not know they are on it.
5. Fees not stated clearly before submission, or requested only after acceptance.
6. No clear policies on peer review, retractions, conflicts of interest, or archiving.

How to check:

1. **Think. Check. Submit.** checklist (https://thinkchecksubmit.org).
2. **DOAJ** listing for open-access journals.
3. **COPE** membership (https://publicationethics.org), and whether the journal actually follows COPE guidance.
4. **NLM Catalog** for MEDLINE status; the publisher's membership of recognized associations (for example OASPA).
5. Ask a librarian or an experienced colleague when in doubt.

## 5. Preprints

1. **What they are.** A preprint is a manuscript posted publicly before peer review, on a server such as medRxiv (https://www.medrxiv.org) or SciELO Preprints (https://preprints.scielo.org).
2. **ICMJE position.** Posting a preprint does not prevent a journal from considering the complete report, but it may influence a journal's interest or priority. Authors must tell the journal when the work has been posted as a preprint, give the link, and later update the preprint to point to the published article. Choose a server that identifies preprints as not peer-reviewed and requires disclosures and funding. When citing a preprint, mark it as a preprint, and cite the published version once it exists (ICMJE Recommendations, "Overlapping Publications").
3. **Journal policies vary.** Most major medical journals accept submissions that are posted as preprints; some restrict it or have conditions (for example, not posting revised versions during review). Check the target journal's policy before posting.
4. **Clinical caution.** Results that could change patient care attract attention before peer review; make limitations explicit in the preprint.
5. **Prior publication and meetings.** Conference abstracts and posters are not prior publication for most journals, but must be disclosed. Duplicate publication of the same results is not acceptable.

## 6. Decision Checklist

1. Scope and article type match; recent related articles identified.
2. Readership is the one that can act on the finding.
3. Indexing verified at the source (NLM Catalog, SciELO, DOAJ).
4. APC, waivers, licence, and funder requirements acceptable.
5. No predatory warning signs; Think. Check. Submit. passed.
6. Preprint policy compatible with your plans.
7. Two alternative journals chosen, with their profiles ready if you use the build kit (`templates/build-kit/journals/`).
